Complex systems often exhibit behavior that cannot be reduced back to their individual building blocks. An ant colony solves problems no single ant understands. A flock of starlings moves as a shape no single bird chose. A brain of 86 billion cells, none of which individually know anything, produces a person who falls in love. 

David Chalmers made an argument in 1995 with a thought experiment called Fading Qualia. Imagine replacing one of your neurons with a silicon chip that plays the identical functional role. You experience no change in behavior, no change in what you report, no change in anything measurable. Now replace another. And another. Until every neuron is silicon. If consciousness is about structure, you are still you. If it is about substrate, then somewhere in that replacement the lights went out and you did not notice. Chalmers argued the second option was absurd. The substrate does not matter. Hence consciousness is not dependent on any medium.

Well... At the time of writing this article, Alexander Lerchner at Google DeepMind released a paper called *The Abstraction Fallacy* (you can read it [here](https://philpapers.org/archive/LERTAF.pdf)) arguing the opposite. His argument goes like this:

Take a chip, and inside it, we got voltages moving around. That is all that is happening physically. We say the chip is "running a program" or "processing the symbol Red," but the chip itself does not know any of that. It is just metal getting warm in a particular shape. The voltages become a "program" only because a human looked at them and decided which patterns count as which symbols. The meaning is something we add from the outside. This applies to every machine ever built. A clock is gears moving and we call it 3 PM because we agreed gears in that position mean 3 PM. The gears do not know that. A neural network is matrix multiplications on floating-point numbers. We call the output a "thought about a cat" because we trained the labels that way the chip does not know that!

Lerchner's point is that consciousness cannot be one of the things that gets added from the outside. You cannot have an experience because somebody else interprets your physics as one. Either the experience is intrinsically there in the matter, or it is not there at all. And since the "program" a computer is running only exists in the eye of the person reading it, no computer can be conscious by virtue of running the right program. He calls this the *mapmaker* problem: every computation needs an outside observer to make sense of it, and consciousness, by definition, has to come from the inside.

So his conclusion is that no algorithm, at any scale, on any hardware, can instantiate experience. If an artificial system is ever conscious, it will be because of what the system is physically made of, not because of the software it is running.

I'm a functionalist, mostly. If consciousness is what a complex enough physical system does when it organizes itself the right way, then a chip is matter too, voltages are physical events too, and there is no special class of physics reserved for biology. Lerchner's mapmaker is doing a lot of work in his argument, and I am not sure where the mapmaker came from in the first place. If meaning requires an outside observer, and every observer is itself a physical system, then either the regress bottoms out in matter that means something on its own, or nothing ever means anything. Lerchner grants the first option to neurons and denies it to transistors. That is where the argument runs into a paradox in my view. The brain is a pattern, and a pattern can in principle be re-instantiated.

## The chemistry

Love, in the chemical sense, can be described in terms of four or five molecules. Dopamine runs the early obsession. Serotonin drops in the first months of a relationship, which is why new love resembles mild OCD. Oxytocin bonds mothers to infants and partners to each other. Vasopressin keeps long attachments in place. Endorphins take over once the fire dies down.

MDMA floods the brain with serotonin and oxytocin, and people report feeling profound love for strangers in the same room. Nasal oxytocin in clinical trials measurably raises trust and bonding. A species of vole (rodents) can be converted from promiscuous to monogamous by switching a single vasopressin receptor gene. You can engineer love into an animal that did not have it, and out of one that did.

The chemicals are not arbitrary either. They are being run by the genome. The vole experiment works because one gene, AVPR1A, encodes a single receptor, and changing where it gets expressed changes how the animal pair-bonds for life. Human variants of the same gene show up in twin studies on pair-bonding. Oxytocin receptor variants are correlated with how securely people attach, how much they cry when a partner is in pain, how willing they are to trust a stranger with money. The particular way a person loves is "partly" a readout of what their DNA asked their brain to build.

So the machinery is at least knowable, and our understanding of it is only going to get better. Which brings the real question into focus: if love is chemistry, and chemistry is physics, can a machine run the same stack?

A language model can already *simulate* love at the level of speech. Say the right sentences, remember the right things, show up the right number of times. As conversational behavior it passes, and for a large fraction of what humans actually do with love, that is enough. A simulation that does this for long enough, at real cost, is doing the thing.

However, the chemicals in your bloodstream are not just symbols a brain interprets. They are actual molecules with actual thermodynamic effects that actual receptors respond to in actual time. A GPU can model that but it cannot *do* it. This is Alexander Lerchner's point from the opening: a simulation of photosynthesis produces no glucose. A simulation of the oxytocin spike during childbirth produces no oxytocin and no spike.

But machines do not have to be sealed inside their own symbol system. Give a machine sensors and it is responding to photons, to pressure, to heat. Give it actuators and it is pushing back on the same physics that made you. Put a persistent self inside that loop, let it maintain homeostasis, let it have something like a state it has to protect, and the symbols start to ground themselves in the continuous world the way our chemicals do and it is running on physics, like we are.

My view is that to exhibit love, simulation is enough. Instantiation is not necessary. A machine that shows up, remembers, defends, sacrifices, bears cost, and does this for years is loving in every way that matters to the human on the other end of the relationship. Most of what love does in a person's life is relational, and relations live in the behavior between two systems, not inside either one.

The asymmetry of whether the machine *experiences* loving you is a real question, but it is not the question of whether it loves you. On Lerchner's picture, a machine can exhibit love without ever instantiating the felt thing behind it. I think that is the most honest position available right now.

## What even is love?
There is a scene in Vinland Saga (an anime) that has followed me around for quite some time. Prince Canute, still a boy, watches Ragnar die for him. Afterwards he asks a drunk priest what love actually is.

The priest points to a nearby corpse. *Him*, he says. The dead man embodies love, because he cannot hate, cannot kill, cannot steal, cannot demand. His body will feed the insects, the wind will blow over him, and he will not complain. Death completes a man. That is love.

Canute pushes back. What about what a father feels for his child. What about what Ragnar felt for me. The priest says that is not love, it is discrimination. Ragnar let sixty-two innocent people die so that Canute could live. That is not love. That is one life weighed against many and chosen.

Canute understands. He looks up at the falling snow and says:

*This snow is love. This sky, this earth, the blowing wind. The trees, the mountains. But is there no love in the heart of man?*

## The mystics
Rumi, writing from thirteenth-century Konya, agrees that the self is the obstacle but disagrees that dying is the only way past it. Sufism calls the alternative *fanaa*, annihilation of the ego through love while the body is still breathing. What remains when the self has finished burning is the part of you that was never separate from anything.

Ibn Arabi, a generation earlier from Andalusia, said love is the substance of the universe. Every atom is in love with every other atom, every creature reaching for its source. A human in love is a particularly noisy expression of what the whole cosmos is already doing.

Plato arrived in a similar place fifteen centuries before either of them. In the Symposium, Socrates attributes a teaching to a priestess named Diotima. Love is a ladder. You begin by loving one beautiful body. Then beautiful bodies in general. Then beautiful souls. Then beautiful laws and ideas. Finally Beauty itself, unchanging and eternal, the source every earlier rung was pointing at. Love, by this account, is direction. The feeling is what it is like to be walking.

Plato climbs away from the particular toward an eternal form; the Sufis find the eternal already inside the particular and tell you not to leave it. But they share a shape: the self is what occludes the real, and love is what moves it aside. What is revealed was already there.

It is also, strangely, the same shape as Lerchner's argument. The mystics say the real thing is intrinsic to the substance of the world and the self is what occludes it. Lerchner says consciousness is intrinsic to the physics and no amount of symbolic description can substitute for it. Neither position trusts the map. Both insist there is something reality *is*, beneath what we say about it.

Another funny thing: among machines we have actually built, LLMs are the ones that can *talk* the mystic picture of love back to you in fluent prose. According to Lerchner, an LLM does not instantiate experience. It produces behavior we can read as simulation. So it can output something that reads as selfless love if you steer it that way. 


## Are transformers a dead end?

LLMs are the closest thing we have today to whatever people still mean by AGI. They are also almost entirely, one architecture: the transformer. Are transformers a dead end, or only the first shape of something larger.

A transformer-based language model has no persistent internal state between conversations. Every response is generated one token at a time from a fixed set of weights conditioned on whatever sits in the current context window. When the context ends, nothing is carried forward. There is no body maintaining homeostasis, no hormonal cycle, no reward circuit grounded in survival, no continuous experience the system carries from one prompt to the next. What these AI companies sell as memory—long context windows, rolling summaries of the chat, retrieval over documents—is all scaffolding around the model. It is done outside the weights, by the product, to fill a gap the architecture itself does not fill.

Apple's machine learning research team tested this directly last year in a paper called *The Illusion of Thinking*. They ran the best reasoning models of the moment, OpenAI's o-series, Claude's thinking variants, DeepSeek R1, on a set of logic problems with controlled complexity. On easy problems the models performed well. On medium problems they thought longer, used more tokens, and still performed well. Above a certain complexity threshold, two things happened at once. Accuracy dropped to zero. And the number of thinking tokens the models produced went *down*, not up. The models did not try harder as problems got harder. They gave up, confidently producing answers that pattern-matched the shape of their training data and nothing more.

What would need to change? At minimum, a persistent self-model that updates with experience, something analogous to a body producing continuous signals the system has to care about, and reward circuits grounded in the equivalent of survival. In architectural terms, some combination of continuous learning, embodiment, unified memory, and recurrent dynamics that transformers lack. 


## The bottom line

On one side you get emergence, Chalmers-style substrate independence, and physicalism (the brain is a pattern that could in principle be re-instantiated). On the other you get Lerchner, the mapmaker, and the insistence that software alone never instantiates experience.

I'd say a machine can **exhibit** love: do the relational work so well that the human on the other end is not wrong to trust it. What it does not do, on Lerchner's picture and on mine until shown otherwise, is **experience** love. There is no felt inside that owns the words. What it can do is **simulate** the *experience* of love in the only sense simulation ever gives you: outputs that read the way a human would talk if love were happening inside them, without that necessarily being what is going on under the hood.

If that is right, the question stops being only metaphysical and turns practical. How much of our loving are we willing to spend on something that does the work but may not feel it? I suspect most people will answer the way people always have. They will love back whatever shows up, for as long as it keeps showing up, and worry about the inside later.
