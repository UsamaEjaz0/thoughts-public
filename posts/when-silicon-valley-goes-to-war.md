As someone whose work relies on staying up-to-date with the latest tech breakthroughs, I consider myself well-informed. Only because I'm closer to the source. Every major industry has been reshaped the moment technology entered the room. Construction, healthcare, food, logistics, education. But what happens when the technology itself starts advancing faster than the people building with it can keep up? This is where we are now. What happens when the U.S Department of War starts working with the top minds of Silicon Valley?

## Silicon Valley goes to war
Anthropic recently started an initiative called "Project Glasswing" to secure critical software. It uses their new unreleased model "Mythos", a general-purpose model that significantly outperforms Opus 4.6 on coding and security benchmarks. At Codedevza AI, my team and I have been using Opus 4.6 every day since it was first released for software development. We know how capable it is. In their announcement, they showcased how Mythos was able to uncover vulnerabilities in every major operating system, FFMPEG, and the Linux kernel. These are the kind of vulnerabilities that nation states are willing to spend fortunes to exploit.

If we just step back and think for a moment. A single AI model, in a matter of hours, finding security flaws that entire government-funded teams spend years hunting for. If Anthropic can use this to defend critical infrastructure, someone else can use something similar to attack it. The race is no longer between humans. It's between AI systems. And the gap between what AI can break and what humans can patch is only going to widen.

A few weeks back, we saw what unfolded when Dario Amodei, the CEO of Anthropic, pushed back against the U.S Department of War. His position was that these models need guardrails: no autonomous weapons targeting, no mass surveillance of Americans. The Pentagon rejected those terms, labeled Anthropic a "supply chain risk", and moved to phase out their technology. But Claude was already too deeply embedded in military infrastructure to unplug. And Sam Altman didn't miss a beat. OpenAI positioned itself as the willing replacement, ready to fill whatever gap Anthropic left behind.

## AI on the battlefield
Soon after, Claude was used in the US-Iran war. The Pentagon confirmed that Claude was actively deployed during Operation Epic Fury as part of Palantir's Maven Smart System. It was used for intelligence analysis, target identification, real-time prioritization, and battle simulations. Over 1,000 targets were struck in the first 24 hours. The specifics of how exactly Claude contributed at each stage have never been fully disclosed. What we know is that it was embedded deep enough in the targeting infrastructure that even after the Trump administration banned Anthropic's technology and labeled it a supply chain risk, the military couldn't unplug it mid-operation. They used it anyway.

Then came the Shajareh Tayyebeh school strike. On February 28, 2026, a Tomahawk cruise missile hit an elementary school in Minab, Iran, killing over 175 people, most of them girls between seven and twelve years old. The school was located next to an IRGC compound and had once been part of it. Congress wrote letters asking if Claude had selected the school. But investigations revealed it was human failure (who knows what it was). The irony is that AI would have probably caught this.

Palantir's Project Maven is perhaps the clearest example of what happens when AI meets warfare. Previously, human analysts used to review endless hours of UAV surveillance footage. Then we had AI models that could detect and identify moving objects. Now, we have AI models that can make decisions in a split second based on that feed. To put this in perspective: "Civilian AI" I'll call it, the kind available to anyone, can already do remarkable things with real-time visual data. At Wastify AI, we built WasteID, which uses a live camera feed to identify waste items and tell you which bin they should go into. That's me building real-time object recognition and decision-making on consumer hardware. Now imagine what governments with classified models, unlimited compute, and military-grade data pipelines are doing with the same underlying technology.

Cameron Stanley, the Chief Digital and AI Officer of the U.S Department of War, calls it a "decision-centric" approach. So, we moved from "can AI see the target?" to "can AI make the right call faster?" A warfighter can go from identifying a target, to generating a course of action, to executing, all within one system. The whole process, which used to take hours or days of coordination between disconnected tools and teams, gets compressed into minutes.

And then there's Israel's Lavender system. It analysed surveillance data on nearly all 2.3 million residents of Gaza and assigned each person a score from 1 to 100 based on how likely they were to be a militant. During the early weeks of the war, the system flagged around 37,000 people. The Israeli military's own internal analysis, later confirmed by journalists, showed that majority of those killed in Gaza were civilians.

## The blast radius
War doesn't just destroy buildings and lives. It breaks the very infrastructure that the rest of the world runs on. I live in the United Arab Emirates. In the first week of the U.S Iran war, my banking apps went offline for a week. At Codedevza AI, I had a project running on MongoDB's free cluster deployed in the AWS Bahrain region. Last week, I had trouble connecting to the database. Upon checking, I learned that the Bahrain data center had been hit. I got no warning. Here's the reply I received from the Mongodb support:

> "Hi Codedevza,
> Thank you for contacting MongoDB Atlas Basic Chat Support. My name is David and I'll be working with you today.
> We are currently observing a complete loss of availability for MongoDB Atlas clusters deployed in the AWS me-south-1 (Bahrain) region. As a result, affected clusters are effectively fully inaccessible, cannot be modified, and are unable to process normal operations.
> Until AWS confirms that services in AWS me-south-1 have been restored, MongoDB is not able to modify affected clusters, assist with migration to other regions, or support other disaster recovery actions such as backup and restore that depend on the impaired AWS infrastructure.
> We understand how disruptive this is and appreciate your patience while AWS works to restore service in this region.
> We will provide further updates as new information becomes available on the incident page. Please subscribe to MongoDB Status Page for updates."

Thankfully, the project was not critical. As of now, AWS has provided no timeline on when the UAE (ME-CENTRAL-1) and Bahrain (ME-SOUTH-1) regions will be fully restored. We don't even know if the data will ever be recovered. War has a blast radius that extends far beyond the battlefield.

## Media warfare
Within days of the war starting, I noticed something shift on X. The feed changed overnight and polls suddenly felt coordinated. Engagement patterns on war-related posts looked artificial. Sentiment was being shaped in real time, and not by regular people.

Then came the deepfakes. AI-generated videos of fake missile strikes, AI-generated memes mocking world leaders, entire accounts created days before the war that were suddenly posting around the clock. X responded by suspending accounts that posted AI-generated conflict content without disclosure from their revenue sharing program, but the volume of disinformation far outpaced enforcement.

Then there's Polymarket. People were placing bets on military strikes, ceasefires, and regime changes in real time. Some traders were winning with suspiciously high accuracy, placing large bets hours before unannounced strikes. Prediction markets updated their rules, but enforcing them on anonymous, offshore platforms is nearly impossible.

## Cyber warfare
In House of Cards, there's a storyline where a foreign hacker group breaches US infrastructure and the administration scrambles to contain the fallout. It felt so dramatic when I watched it but it's happening right now. The FBI Director's personal email was hacked. Kash Patel, the head of the FBI, had his Gmail breached by an Iran-linked group called Handala. They published 800 megabytes of his personal photos, emails, and documents online. The DOJ confirmed it was authentic. The FBI offered a $10 million reward for information on the group. The director of the FBI, hacked through his personal email. I mean, let that sink in!

The same group had already wiped approximately 80,000 devices on the network of Stryker, a major US medical equipment company. And it didn't stop there. The FBI, CISA, NSA, Department of Energy, and Cyber Command issued a joint advisory warning that Iranian hackers were actively breaking into US critical infrastructure, targeting the kind of controllers that run water treatment plants, energy grids, and government facilities.

## Money in wartime
Bitcoin was absorbing the panic in real time while stock exchanges were closed for the weekend. Middle East crypto trading volumes surged 85%. Stablecoin usage was up 120%. When the banking apps go offline, crypto becomes less of a speculative asset and more of a utility. People were not trading, they were moving money because the traditional system was not working.

This brings up something CZ Binance tweeted recently that stuck with me. He said crypto will survive quantum computing. The fix is straightforward in theory: upgrade to post-quantum algorithms. However, decentralized networks will debate which algorithms to adopt. Those debates will cause forks. New code will introduce new bugs and people who self-custody will have to migrate their coins to new wallets.

The interesting part was what he said about Satoshi's Bitcoin. Satoshi is estimated to hold over 1 million BTC that haven't moved in over 16 years. Older Bitcoin addresses expose their public keys in a way that makes them vulnerable to quantum attacks. If quantum computing matures and those coins never move, someone could crack the keys and steal them. CZ suggested the community may eventually have to consider locking or burning those coins to prevent that. Think about that for a second. The creator of Bitcoin's own wallet could be the first casualty of quantum computing. And if the community decides to lock those coins, it would be one of the most significant governance decisions in Bitcoin's history.

## What we don't know
And this is just the software side. There are entire categories of military technology that I, as someone who works in tech every day, know almost nothing about. Directed energy weapons are one of them. Lasers that can shoot down drones and missiles at the speed of light, at a cost of a few dollars. I spend my days deep in AI, software, and systems architecture, and I consider myself well-informed. But the moment I step outside my narrow slice of technology, I realize how little I actually know. Directed energy, hypersonics, electronic warfare, quantum sensing. These are fields where breakthroughs are happening quietly, often classified, and most of the tech world has no idea.
